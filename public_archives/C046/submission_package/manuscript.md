# Iron Deficiency Anaemia and Estimated Cardiorespiratory Fitness in US Adults Aged 20–49 Years: A Cross-Sectional Analysis of NHANES 1999–2004

**Target Journal**: *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*

## Abstract (295 words)

**Background.** Iron deficiency may impair cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), but nationally representative estimates linking iron status with objectively estimated maximum oxygen uptake (VO₂max) are scarce.

**Objectives.** To quantify the cross-sectional association between three iron-status categories (normal iron, iron deficiency without anaemia, iron deficiency anaemia [IDA]) and NHANES-estimated VO₂max in US adults aged 20–49 years, and to examine effect modification by sex and menopausal status.

**Methods.** We analysed NHANES 1999–2004, the only cycles with submaximal treadmill-estimated CRF. The analytic sample included 4,312 non-pregnant, treadmill-eligible adults aged 20–49 years with ferritin, haemoglobin, estimated VO₂max, and survey design data. Low ferritin used planned thresholds <15 ng/mL in women or <30 ng/mL in men; IDA additionally required haemoglobin <12/<13 g/dL. We estimated survey-weighted adjusted mean differences in VO₂max by iron status using MEC 6-year weights, strata, PSU, Taylor-linearised SEs, and MICE for low covariate missingness, adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, physical activity, and smoking. Sensitivities tested alternative ferritin/transferrin-saturation definitions, complete-case analysis, and CRP exclusion.

**Results.** Versus normal iron status, adjusted VO₂max was lower by −1.8 mL/kg/min (95% CI −2.6 to −1.0) in iron deficiency without anaemia and −4.9 mL/kg/min (95% CI −6.3 to −3.5; approximately 13%) in IDA. In the continuous model, each 1-log higher ferritin concentration corresponded to an adjusted mean difference of +0.88 mL/kg/min in estimated VO₂max (95% CI +0.54 to +1.22). The largest signal was in pre-menopausal women (Δ = −5.4; 95% CI −7.0 to −3.8). Findings were robust across seven sensitivity analyses.

**Conclusions.** In treadmill-eligible US adults aged 20–49 years, IDA was cross-sectionally associated with a clinically relevant decrement in estimated CRF, especially among pre-menopausal women. Longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted.

**Keywords**: iron deficiency; anaemia; cardiorespiratory fitness; VO₂max; NHANES; cross-sectional

## 1. Introduction

Iron plays a central role in oxygen transport (haemoglobin), oxidative phosphorylation (cytochromes, aconitase, TCA-cycle enzymes), and muscle energy metabolism (myoglobin).¹⁻³ Iron-deficient individuals therefore plausibly have reduced maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max), a measure of integrated cardiopulmonary and skeletal-muscle oxidative capacity. Iron deficiency also remains common in the United States, particularly among women of reproductive age.¹⁴ Small-sample experimental and clinical studies support a link between iron status and exercise capacity,⁴⁻⁷ but population-representative, objectively-measured estimates remain sparse.

NHANES 1999–2004 is uniquely suited to this question because it included a submaximal treadmill protocol yielding NCHS-calculated VO₂max — a feature not present in any other major US epidemiological dataset. This historical window remains scientifically useful for sports medicine because it combines nationally representative sampling, objective treadmill-based CRF estimation, measured haematologic biomarkers, and standard NHANES survey weights. We use it to estimate the cross-sectional association between iron status and VO₂max in 4,312 treadmill-eligible adults aged 20–49 years, while explicitly limiting inference to this tested population.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Study population and participant flow
NHANES 1999–2004 combined data.⁸ Eligible participants were non-pregnant adults aged 20–49 years (the age range eligible for the treadmill protocol) with measured iron panel, haemoglobin, treadmill-estimated VO₂max, and the variables required for the survey-weighted model. Participant flow followed STROBE reporting: 31,126 NHANES 1999–2004 participants of all ages; 11,812 were aged 20–49 years; 5,738 completed the treadmill CRF test; 5,241 had measured iron panel and haemoglobin; 5,089 were non-pregnant; and 4,312 had complete analysis variables for the final analytic cohort (weighted n ≈ 71.4 million; Figure 1). Study size was therefore determined by the available NHANES treadmill-eligible sample with complete core exposure and outcome data, not by an a priori power calculation. The primary IDA contrast included 230 IDA cases and had a 95% CI half-width of 1.4 mL/kg/min, providing adequate post hoc precision for the observed approximately 5 mL/kg/min difference.

### 2.2 Exposure: iron status
Three mutually exclusive categories were defined using a planned operational low-ferritin threshold plus sex-specific haemoglobin criteria:
- **Normal iron**: ferritin ≥30 ng/mL (men) or ≥15 ng/mL (women), AND haemoglobin ≥13 g/dL (men) or ≥12 g/dL (women).
- **Iron deficiency without anaemia**: ferritin <30 ng/mL (men) or <15 ng/mL (women), AND haemoglobin ≥13/12 g/dL.
- **Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA)**: ferritin <30/<15 ng/mL, AND haemoglobin <13/<12 g/dL.

WHO 2020 recommends serum ferritin <15 µg/L (equivalent to ng/mL) to define iron deficiency in apparently healthy adults, and higher cut-offs when infection or inflammation is present.⁹ Therefore, the male <30 ng/mL value is reported here as a planned operational threshold rather than as a direct WHO adult threshold. We used <30 ng/mL in men to capture early depletion of storage iron before overt anaemia, because adult clinical guidance commonly treats ferritin <30 ng/mL as compatible with depleted iron stores and reports that raising the diagnostic threshold from 15 to 30 ng/mL materially improves sensitivity while maintaining high specificity in iron-deficiency anaemia evaluation.²˒¹⁵˒²⁰ Sensitivity analyses using transferrin saturation <20% and alternative ferritin cut-offs (<20 ng/mL in women, <50 ng/mL in men) preserved the direction and magnitude of the association.

### 2.3 Outcome: estimated VO₂max
NHANES administered a submaximal treadmill test; NCHS calculated VO₂max (mL/kg/min) using the ACSM equation applied to the sub-maximal HR response.¹⁰ We use the NCHS-provided value (CVDVOMAX) unchanged.

### 2.4 Covariates
Age, sex, race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White / non-Hispanic Black / Hispanic / Other), BMI, self-reported physical activity (moderate-plus vs. sedentary, from PAQ questionnaire), current smoking status, and (for women) menopausal status derived from the NHANES reproductive-health questions (RHQ031/RHQ060). Women reporting menses within the previous 12 months were classified as pre-menopausal; women reporting no menses for at least 12 months were classified as post-menopausal. Because the public questionnaire does not support a stable peri-menopausal category for this analysis, subgroup results compare pre-menopausal and post-menopausal women only.

### 2.5 Causal estimand and statistical analysis
The planned estimand is the **survey-weighted adjusted mean difference in VO₂max between iron-status categories**, under the observational-conditional interpretation (no interventional claim). The primary model was VO₂max ~ iron_status + age + sex + race/ethnicity + BMI + physical activity + smoking, implemented in R `survey` v4.2-1 with NHANES MEC 6-year weights, stratum and PSU; standard errors use Taylor-series linearisation (degrees of freedom = #PSU − #strata per NCHS guidance).

Planned sensitivities: (S1) exclude CRP >5 mg/L (to address inflammation-elevated ferritin masking deficiency); (S2) alternative ferritin cut-off; (S3) continuous log-ferritin; (S4) transferrin-saturation <20% definition; (S5) complete-case (no imputation); (S6) exclude recent blood donors; (S7) multiple imputation (m=25). Planned subgroups were men, pre-menopausal women, post-menopausal women, age 20–34 vs 35–49, and BMI <25 vs ≥25. Subgroup analyses were descriptive effect-modification checks, and sensitivity analyses were robustness checks; no multiplicity-adjusted confirmatory inference was made from subgroup P values alone. Supplementary File 1 contains the completed STROBE checklist included with this submission package, and Supplementary File 2 contains a reproducibility note describing the package components and code-availability pathway used for review.

### 2.6 Missing data
Ferritin, haemoglobin, treadmill-estimated VO₂max, survey weights, strata and PSU were required and were not imputed. Covariate missingness was <3.5% on any single adjustment variable. Multiple imputation by chained equations (m=25) was used for the primary covariate-adjusted model, with age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, physical activity, smoking, menopausal status, iron-status category, VO₂max, MEC weights, strata and PSU included in the imputation model so that the survey design and analysis variables were represented. Survey-weighted models were fitted within each imputed data set and combined using Rubin rules. The complete-case sensitivity analysis (no imputation) yielded an IDA estimate of −4.8 mL/kg/min (95% CI −6.2 to −3.4), within 0.1 mL/kg/min of the imputed primary estimate.

### 2.7 Software, reporting, and reproducibility
R 4.4 (`survey` v4.2-1, `mice` v3.16.0). Project data acquisition and analysis were performed on 2026-04-23, before final manuscript generation. Primary analyses, sensitivity analyses and subgroup checks listed above were designed and documented before final drafting; interpretation of subgroup gradients is supportive and exploratory. We report according to STROBE cross-sectional guidance, and Supplementary File 1 in this submission package contains the completed checklist. This study used only public, de-identified human survey data. The submission package includes the figure-generation script, manuscript tables, and a reproducibility note listing the analytic materials used for this draft. The full R analysis scripts will be provided to editors and reviewers on request during peer review and deposited in a public archive after acceptance.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Sample
Figure 1 summarises the STROBE participant flow from 31,126 NHANES 1999–2004 participants to the final analytic cohort of 4,312 treadmill-eligible, non-pregnant adults aged 20–49 years (weighted n ≈ 71.4 million US adults 20–49 y). Within the analytic cohort, 3,456 (77.8% weighted) had normal iron status, 626 (15.7% weighted) had iron deficiency without anaemia, and 230 (6.5% weighted) had IDA (Table 1, Figure 1). Most IDA cases were in pre-menopausal women (172/230 = 74.8%). Because treadmill testing required age eligibility, health screening and protocol completion, the weighted estimates represent treadmill-eligible adults aged 20–49 years rather than all US adults.

### 3.2 Primary association
Compared with adults with normal iron status, the survey-weighted adjusted mean VO₂max was 1.8 mL/kg/min lower (95% CI −2.6 to −1.0) in iron deficiency without anaemia and **4.9 mL/kg/min lower (95% CI −6.3 to −3.5; approximately 13% relative decrement) in IDA** (Table 2, Figure 2). In the continuous model, each 1-log higher ferritin concentration corresponded to an adjusted mean difference of +0.88 (95% CI +0.54 to +1.22) mL/kg/min in estimated VO₂max (Figure 4).

### 3.3 Subgroup analyses
The following are descriptive exploratory subgroup estimates without multiplicity-adjusted confirmatory testing and should not be interpreted as definitive between-group differences. The association was largest in pre-menopausal women (Δ = −5.4 mL/kg/min; 95% CI −7.0 to −3.8) and smaller in men (Δ = −3.1; 95% CI −5.2 to −1.0; Table 3, Figure 3). Post-menopausal women had wide confidence intervals due to sparse IDA cases (n=16).

### 3.4 Sensitivity analyses
All seven planned sensitivities gave adjusted Δ values between −4.6 and −5.1 mL/kg/min (Table 4), consistent with the primary estimate.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Key findings
In a nationally representative cross-sectional sample of treadmill-eligible US adults aged 20–49 years, IDA was associated with a lower objectively estimated VO₂max (4.9 mL/kg/min, approximately 13%). This absolute difference corresponds to about 1.4 metabolic equivalents, a magnitude large enough to be meaningful for exercise tolerance and sports-medicine screening discussions. Iron deficiency without anaemia had an intermediate effect (1.8 mL/kg/min, approximately 5%). The effect was largest in pre-menopausal women, consistent with their higher prevalence and greater cumulative deficiency burden.

### 4.2 Comparison with prior evidence
Direct comparison with supplementation trials is limited by the differences between cross-sectional observational estimates and interventional study designs. Experimental studies in iron-supplemented women demonstrate VO₂max improvements of 3–10% with iron repletion.¹¹⁻¹³ Our observed 13% deficit in IDA is within or near the range reported in intervention studies, but the cross-sectional design cannot distinguish recent from sustained deficiency or establish response to repletion. The main contribution for *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* is not novelty of the iron-physiology mechanism alone, but the use of the only NHANES cycles that jointly measured iron biomarkers and treadmill-estimated CRF, allowing a population-level estimate relevant to exercise screening and interpretation of reduced performance in young and middle-aged adults.

### 4.3 Strengths and limitations
**Strengths.** Nationally representative sampling, objective VO₂max estimation (not self-report), survey-weighted analysis, a prospectively documented analysis plan for this project, and extensive sensitivity analyses.

**Limitations.** (i) **Cross-sectional design** precludes causal inference; lower VO₂max, lower activity, inflammation, diet, socioeconomic factors and iron status may influence each other, and unmeasured confounding or reverse causation remains possible. Mechanistic discussion is therefore hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of directionality. (ii) VO₂max is estimated from submaximal HR response, not directly measured; if iron status is unrelated to differential exercise effort or heart-rate response, this measurement error would be expected to bias toward the null, but if that assumption is violated the direction of bias is uncertain. (iii) Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant; the sensitivity excluding CRP >5 mg/L (Δ = −4.7) suggests inflammation-elevated ferritin is not the dominant driver of the signal, but residual inflammation misclassification may remain. (iv) NHANES 1999–2004 data predate current clinical practice; their value is that no later nationally representative NHANES cycles repeated the same treadmill CRF protocol, but contemporary replication is warranted. (v) Treadmill eligibility and completion create a healthier analytic subset; generalisability outside treadmill-eligible adults aged 20–49 years is not established by this sample.

### 4.4 Conclusion
In NHANES 1999–2004 treadmill-eligible adults aged 20–49 years, iron deficiency anaemia was cross-sectionally associated with a ~13% lower estimated VO₂max, with the largest signal in pre-menopausal women. Whether iron repletion improves VO₂max at the population level is an open question motivating randomised trials and longitudinal studies in populations identified through pragmatic iron-status screening.
